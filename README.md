# Improve Hostel Meal Nutrition (BRAM Project)

A student-led business analysis project at JAGSoM to improve the nutritional quality of hostel food at IFIM Hostel.

## The problem
Students at IFIM Hostel eat all three meals in the mess, but many felt the food had too few vegetables, too little fruit and too little fibre. We also observed that about 5–10% of served food was thrown away, because students took it, tasted it and found it not up to the mark. The hostel pays Sodexo, the food contractor, a fixed budget, and that budget is confidential, so asking for more money was not an option.

## How we approached it
- Mapped the stakeholders: students, hostel secretary, hostel management, college management and Sodexo.
- Ran a student survey to find out how often students get vegetables and fruit, what they would like added, and whether they would eat more vegetables if they were cooked differently.
- Turned the findings into business requirements, use cases, and AS-IS / TO-BE process diagrams.
- Planned the work using a business case, a business analysis work plan and a business requirements document.

## The solution
Redesign the menu within Sodexo's existing budget instead of asking for extra funds:
- Serve more of the vegetables and fruit students said they would actually eat.
- Change the cooking styles (fry, gravy, mixed vegetables) so the food tastes different, not just more of the same.
- Add a simple quality check before food is served.
- Keep measuring waste and student satisfaction after the change.

We shared our report with the hostel secretary and management, and Sodexo introduced a new menu with more fibre and fruit and changed cooking styles. Early feedback is positive: students say they are getting more fibre and nutrition, and Sodexo reports less food waste. Taste still needs improvement, so we treat this as an ongoing process rather than a finished result.

## A note on the survey numbers
Our student survey was still open while we were working. The Business Case was prepared when we had 36 responses, so it uses those figures. A few more responses came in afterwards, so the later material, including the final presentation, uses the full set of 44 responses. The percentages differ slightly between the early and later documents for this reason. The findings point the same way in both.
